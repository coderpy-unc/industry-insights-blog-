## The labour crisis in warehousing — structural, not cyclical

Global warehousing and logistics faces a 35% labour shortage in key markets. In Singapore, the United States, Western Europe, and Japan, the gap between available warehouse workers and open positions has widened every year since 2020 and shows no sign of narrowing.

The attrition problem compounds the shortage. Blue-collar, non-unionised warehouse roles — picking, packing, replenishment, goods-in sorting — experience annual attrition rates above 50% in most major markets. Half the workforce leaves every year. The cost of recruiting, onboarding, and training a warehouse picker, only to lose them within 12 months, is significant and recurring. In high-volume operations, the HR and training overhead from attrition consumes management bandwidth that should be focused on operational improvement.

Two structural forces are driving this and will not reverse:

**Generational shift** — Gen Z workers are entering the workforce with a clear preference away from physically demanding, repetitive, shift-based manual roles. The pipeline of workers willing to take warehouse picking positions at market wages is shrinking. Raising wages helps at the margin but does not solve a preference-driven structural shortage.

**Demographic aging** — in Singapore, Japan, and parts of Europe, the existing warehouse workforce skews heavily toward older workers approaching retirement. The average age in many warehouse operations exceeds 45. The replacement cohort is smaller and less willing to take the same roles. This retirement wave will accelerate over the next decade regardless of wage levels or working conditions.

The result is an operations environment where labour is simultaneously scarce, expensive, and unreliable — and where the traditional response (hire more people) is producing diminishing returns.

## What robots are doing in warehouses today

The robotics applications that are operational at scale in enterprise warehousing — not pilots, not demonstrations, but running production:

**Goods-to-person (GTP) systems** — autonomous mobile robots carry shelf units to stationary pickers at ergonomic workstations, eliminating the 60–70% of a picker's time historically spent walking between locations. GTP systems have demonstrated sustained picking rate improvements of 2–4× over conventional pick-and-walk operations, with error rates below 0.1%.

**Autonomous replenishment** — robots move goods from receiving docks to storage locations, and from bulk storage to picking faces, without human direction. They work during off-peak periods when human labour is not available or is costly, and they do so at consistent throughput without the fatigue-driven slowdowns that characterise manual replenishment on long shifts.

**Inventory counting and cycle counting** — robots with scanning capability traverse warehouse aisles during off-hours, counting inventory against the WMS record. This eliminates or dramatically reduces the labour-intensive scheduled stock counts that close operations for days at a time and still produce counts with 3–5% error rates.

**Goods-out sorting** — robotic sorting systems handle the high-speed, high-accuracy sorting of outbound parcels and pallets that would otherwise require large teams of manual sorters working multiple shifts.

Across these applications, the documented labour savings range from 25% to 65% of the workforce required for equivalent throughput, depending on the application and the degree of automation.

## The WMS integration requirement

A warehouse robot that cannot communicate with the Warehouse Management System is an expensive guided vehicle — it can move, but it cannot make decisions based on what the operation actually needs done.

WMS integration is what transforms a robot from a labour-reduction device into an operational intelligence layer. When the robot receives task instructions directly from the WMS — pick this SKU from this location, move this pallet to this dock, replenish this face from this bulk location — it operates as a WMS-directed worker, prioritising tasks by the same logic that directs human workers, and reporting completion back to the WMS to maintain inventory accuracy.

The integration requirements are non-trivial. Every WMS has its own data model, API structure, and task assignment logic. The robot's management platform must map between its own task representation and the WMS's — and maintain that mapping as both systems update over time. This is the integration work that most robotics vendors treat as a professional services engagement after the sale. At Sirona, it is built into the Co-Pilot platform's enterprise orchestration layer.

## Pick accuracy and the quality dimension

Labour shortage creates a secondary problem that is less visible but operationally significant: quality degradation. A warehouse operation under staffing pressure fills open positions faster and with less screening than a well-staffed operation. Training is compressed. Supervision is stretched. Pick error rates rise.

A misrouted pick in an e-commerce fulfilment operation costs 5–8× the value of a correct pick when the return, re-processing, and customer service costs are included. In pharmaceutical distribution, a pick error has regulatory consequences beyond cost. In food distribution, it has safety implications.

Robotic systems operating under WMS direction have documented pick accuracy rates above 99.9% — sustained, not peak. This accuracy benefit complements the labour saving and contributes meaningfully to the ROI calculation in accuracy-sensitive operations.
